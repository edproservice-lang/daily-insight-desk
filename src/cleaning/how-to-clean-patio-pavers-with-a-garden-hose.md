## The short answer

Sweep the patio dry. Wet it. Scrub it in sections with a stiff plastic brush and a bucket of water with a little mild detergent. Rinse with a wide spray held low and aimed across the pavers, toward the edge where water already drains. Let it dry, then sweep sand back into any joint that came up short.

That handles dust, pollen, mud, and most of the gray film on a patio that gets used. It does not dissolve set-in oil, and it does not fix moss that keeps coming back because the spot is shady and damp. The hose is the rinse. The brush does the cleaning. Anyone selling the hose as the whole answer is selling the nozzle.

## What a hose can and cannot do

A garden hose brings whatever pressure your house has. A nozzle shapes that water into a fan or a narrower stream. It does not add a pump. The [garden hose nozzle guide](/cleaning/garden-hose-pressure-nozzle/) goes through what that means in practice: a nozzle aims the water you already have, which is plenty to carry loosened dirt off a patio and not enough to peel off anything that has gripped the surface.

That limit is also the point. The Concrete Masonry and Hardscapes Association’s [tech note on cleaning interlocking concrete pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-005/) describes the do-it-yourself version for residential patios and walks plainly: apply the cleaner by hand, scrub, then rinse thoroughly with water from a garden hose. No machine required for a small area.

What water alone moves:

- Dust, pollen, and the crumbs of a weekend.
- Mud that has dried on top, after it is wet long enough to soften.
- Soap and loosened grime you have already scrubbed free.

What water alone leaves:

- Moss and algae holding on in the joints.
- Oil, grease, and rust that have soaked into the surface.
- White haze on newer concrete pavers, which is a deposit, not dirt.

## What you need

- **A hose and a nozzle with a wide fan setting.** A shut-off at the handle saves water while you scrub.
- **A stiff brush.** Plastic or brass bristles. CMHA says steel bristles are not recommended because they break off, rust, and leave stains.
- **A bucket and a mild detergent.** Ordinary dish soap is what most people already have.
- **Dry joint sand,** to top off any joint that loses some during the rinse.
- **Old sheets or plastic bags** if you will use anything stronger than soap near plants.

If you already keep one concentrate for most of the house, check that its label names outdoor stone before you pour it on a patio. The guide to [cleaning tablets and refill sprays](/cleaning/do-cleaning-tablets-replace-spray-bottles/) makes the same argument indoors: the cleaner has to match the surface, not the room.

## How to clean pavers with a hose, step by step

### 1. Clear and sweep it dry

Move the chairs, the grill, and the planters. Sweep leaves, grit, and acorns while everything is dry. Wet leaves smear, and grit under a brush acts like sandpaper.

### 2. Wet the pavers

Give the whole patio an even soak with the fan setting. Concrete is porous. A dry paver drinks the soapy water. A damp one keeps it on the surface, where you can scrub it off.

### 3. Soap one section at a time

Work an area you can scrub before it dries, starting from the high side or from the house outward. On a hot afternoon, that area is small. Early morning or an overcast day gives you more time and fewer soap streaks.

### 4. Scrub across the joints

Push the brush across the pavers and diagonally over the joints. Long strokes straight down a joint line dig sand out. Let the soap sit a few minutes on the dirtiest patches, but do not let it dry.

### 5. Rinse low and wide

Switch to the widest fan. Keep the nozzle low and pointed across the surface, not straight down. Push the dirty water toward the edge that already drains, away from the foundation and, if you can manage it, away from the flower beds. Rinse until the water running off is clear.

### 6. Let it dry, then look

Check the patio the next day, not while it is wet. Wet concrete hides streaks and missed spots. CMHA suggests letting cleaned pavers dry at least 24 hours before judging the results of a cleaner.

## Moss, oil, and the white haze

### Moss and algae

Water slides over moss. Scraping and scrubbing removes it. An Ask Extension answer on [removing moss from concrete](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=820065) adds two points worth keeping: protect nearby plants before you use any household treatment, and remove every bit of moss the rinse leaves behind, because what stays holds water and gives new moss a start. The same answer is blunt about the cause. If the spot is shady and damp, the conditions that grew the moss are still there after you clean it.

If moss is the whole problem, it is its own job. A guide on moss without a pressure washer is listed as upcoming on the [outdoor shelf](/outdoor/).

### Oil and grease

Fresh drips from a grill come up more easily than old ones. Blot or cover a fresh spot with an absorbent, sweep it up, then scrub with detergent and rinse. A black patch that has been there all summer is no longer sitting on top. That is a job for a cleaner labeled for concrete pavers, tested on a hidden spot first, or for a careful pressure washer on a setting that will not mark the surface.

### The white haze on newer pavers

A powdery white film on concrete pavers is usually efflorescence. CMHA explains that it forms when calcium hydroxide in the concrete moves to the surface and reacts with carbon dioxide in the air. It does not affect the strength or durability of the pavers, and much of it can wear away over months. Scrubbing harder will not settle it for good. If you want it gone sooner, CMHA points to a remover made for concrete pavers, used exactly as its label says, with gloves and eye protection.

## Keep the sand in the joints

The sand between pavers is not decoration. It locks them together. Wash it out and the pavers can shift, rock underfoot, and leave gaps that weeds move into.

Three habits protect it. Keep the spray wide. Keep the nozzle low. Aim across the pavers, never straight down a joint. If you see sand moving in the runoff, back off before the joint empties.

When the patio is fully dry, sweep fresh joint sand into any joint that sits low, and follow the sand bag’s directions for setting it. A just-cleaned patio is also the natural moment to decide on a sealer or a joint stabilizer. CMHA notes that cleaning comes first, and that products made specifically for concrete pavers give the best results.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**

- Little risk of scarring the surface or blowing out the joints.
- No machine to rent, fuel, or store.
- Uses a brush, a bucket, and soap you probably own.
- Easy to keep runoff away from beds and the foundation.

**Cons**

- Your arms do the work the pump would have done.
- Slow on a large patio, section by section.
- Will not remove set-in oil, rust, or entrenched moss on its own.
- Spray strength is limited by your house’s water pressure.

## Who it is for

This is for a homeowner with an ordinary paver patio or walk that is dirty rather than damaged: pollen in spring, leaves in fall, a summer of shoes and spilled drinks. An Ask Extension answer on [moss on a patio](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=925903) suggests a stiff brush and a hose, once in early spring and once in late fall. That rhythm is about right for most yards.

Skip it, or plan on more than a hose, if the patio is large, if old oil stains are the real problem, or if the pavers are loose and the joints already empty. That last one is a repair, not a cleaning.
