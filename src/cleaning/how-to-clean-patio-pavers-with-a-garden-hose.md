## The short answer

You can clean patio pavers using a standard garden hose, a stiff broom, dish soap, and manual scrubbing. You do not need a heavy pressure washer for seasonal dirt, surface dust, fresh pollen, or mild algae. High-pressure washing often gouges concrete paver surfaces and blasts away polymeric joint sand, leading to loose blocks and weed growth. A garden hose paired with mild detergent cleans effectively while leaving your paver joint sand intact.

## Why garden hoses work (and where they hit limits)

Concrete and clay pavers are porous materials. Surface dirt settles into small surface pores, but most seasonal grime stays near the surface. A standard garden hose provides 40 to 60 pounds per square inch (psi) of water pressure. That amount of water volume, combined with a mild surfactant like dish soap, softens grime so a stiff brush can sweep it free.

Pressure washers deliver 1,500 to 3,500 psi. While high pressure strips stubborn oil stains or old paint, it also risks pitting paver surfaces and excavating the sand between joints. Using a garden hose avoids those structural trade-offs and keeps pavers locked in place.

However, a garden hose relies on elbow grease. If your patio has deeply embedded motor oil, thick moss mats, or years of baked-in rust, a plain garden hose stream will not lift those stains alone. You must rely on physical scrubbing or specialized pre-treatments recommended by extension service care guides like Penn State Extension to break down grime before rinsing.

## Tools and materials you actually need

To clean pavers without high pressure, gather basic household tools:

- **Garden hose and nozzle:** A hose with a basic adjustable spray nozzle controls stream force and angle.
- **Stiff-bristle broom or deck brush:** Synthetic bristles remove mud and algae without gouging clay or concrete. Avoid wire brushes, which leave tiny steel fragments that rust and stain pavers later.
- **Cleaning solution:** Liquid dish soap mixed with warm water breaks down grease and dirt. For organic growth like mold or mildew, mild oxygen bleach is a safe alternative.
- **Polymeric sand or jointing sand:** Keep a small bag on hand to top off any joints where sand eroded during rinsing.
- **Bucket and garden sprayer:** A 5-gallon bucket works for soap mixes, while a pump sprayer helps spread cleaner evenly across large patio areas.

## Step-by-step: Cleaning pavers with a hose

Cleaning your paver patio with a garden hose involves five practical steps. Follow this process to prevent paver shifting and maximize cleaning efficiency.

### Step 1: Clear the patio surface
Remove all furniture, grills, planters, and lawn decorations. Sweep the entire area with a stiff broom to remove loose leaves, twigs, dirt, and acorns. Clearing loose grit prevents you from dragging abrasive mud across the stones during scrubbing.

### Step 2: Wet the pavers thoroughly
Turn on your hose and wet the pavers thoroughly. Never apply cleaning solutions to dry concrete or clay. Dry pavers absorb soap water deeply into their pores, which can leave behind a dull film or white mineral spots after drying. Wetting the surface creates a liquid barrier that keeps the soap on top where it can work.

### Step 3: Apply the soap solution
Mix 1/4 cup of liquid dish soap into a 5-gallon bucket of warm water. Pour or spray the soapy water evenly across a 10-foot-by-10-foot section of pavers. Working in small sections prevents the cleaning solution from drying out before you scrub and rinse.

### Step 4: Scrub along the paver lines
Scrub the pavers using a stiff-bristle deck brush. Move the brush in multiple directions—across the pavers and diagonally across joint lines. Avoid scrubbing directly along joint lines, as aggressive longitudinal strokes can dig out the sand between the stones. For stubborn grime, let the soapy water sit for 5 to 10 minutes before scrubbing.

### Step 5: Rinse thoroughly at a low angle
Adjust your hose nozzle to a fan or gentle jet spray setting. Hold the nozzle at a 45-degree angle to the surface, roughly 12 to 18 inches above the ground. Rinse the soapy water away from the house toward lawn beds. Direct the stream across paver faces rather than straight down into joint cracks to preserve the sand beneath, following standard home landscape guidelines like those from the [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden).

## Dealing with moss, weeds, and tough stains

Routine hose rinsing handles dust, but specific outdoor stains require focused treatment.

### Organic growth: Moss, mold, and algae
Shaded patio areas collect moss and green algae during damp seasons. To treat organic growth without harsh chemicals, mix 1 cup of oxygen bleach powder per gallon of warm water in a pump sprayer. Coat the moss-covered pavers and let the solution rest for 15 to 20 minutes. Oxygen bleach breaks down organic cell walls without killing surrounding lawn grass or bleaching natural paver dyes. Scrub the area with a deck brush and rinse thoroughly with your hose.

### Oil and grease spots
Barbecue grills and parked vehicles drop oil onto pavers quickly. For fresh oil, apply absorbent cat litter or baking soda immediately to draw up excess liquid. For set-in grease, apply liquid dish soap directly to the dry stain, work it in with a small scrub brush, and let it sit for 30 minutes. Rinse clean with warm water from a bucket, followed by a thorough hose wash.

### Mineral deposits and efflorescence
New concrete pavers often develop a white, powdery film known as efflorescence. This happens when natural salts inside concrete migrate to the surface as moisture evaporates. Washing with water alone will temporarily dissolve salts, but they often return as pavers dry. Allow efflorescence to run its natural course over a few months, or brush it off dry with a stiff broom. Avoid harsh acid-based cleaners unless necessary, as acids permanently alter paver surface texture.

## Protecting your joint sand during rinsing

The sand between your pavers provides lateral stability and keeps blocks from shifting under weight. Improper hose spraying can wash this sand out, creating hollow spaces where weeds take root.

Always keep your spray nozzle angled low—never point it straight down into joints. If you notice sand washing out, pause immediately and lower the water flow or widen the nozzle pattern.

After the patio dries completely, inspect the joints. If sand levels have dropped more than 1/4 inch below the paver edges, sweep fresh polymeric sand into the gaps. Mist the new sand lightly with your hose to set it according to manufacturer directions. Keeping joint sand full prevents weeds from establishing roots and preserves patio alignment.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**
- Low risk of paver etching, surface chipping, or joint sand blowout compared to high-pressure washing.
- Requires no heavy equipment rental, gas engines, or electrical power outlets.
- Uses basic household supplies like dish soap, deck brushes, and standard hoses.
- Safe for surrounding landscaping, garden beds, and lawn edges.
- Cost-effective method for routine seasonal maintenance.

**Cons**
- Requires more physical labor and scrubbing time than power equipment.
- Ineffective on deep, set-in automotive oil stains or heavy rust without pre-treatments.
- Spray strength remains limited by home water pressure and hose connections.
- Requires manual sweeping and scrubbing to loosen embedded dirt.

## Who it is for, and who should skip it

Cleaning pavers with a garden hose is ideal for homeowners maintaining an already well-kept patio, deck surround, or walkway. If your patio suffers from seasonal dust, pollen, bird droppings, or light surface dirt, a hose and brush setup delivers clean results without damaging structural joint sand.

You should skip this manual method if your patio spans thousands of square feet, or if it is covered in thick, multi-year moss growth and embedded automotive fluids. In those cases, direct mechanical scrubbing or professional high-pressure equipment may be necessary to restore the stone surface, followed by a full re-sanding process.

## Maintaining your pavers long term

Keeping pavers clean with a hose requires minimal ongoing effort if you follow a regular maintenance routine. Sweep leaves and organic debris off the patio weekly during autumn. Leaf piles trap moisture, leading to mold stains and tannin discoloration on light concrete.

Rinse the patio once a month during spring and summer to remove pollen before it bakes under direct sunlight. If you use temporary watering attachments or outdoor cleaning tools, like [dissolvable cleaning tablets](/cleaning/do-cleaning-tablets-replace-spray-bottles/) for surface spraying or a simple [garden hose pressure nozzle](/cleaning/garden-hose-pressure-nozzle/) for patio rinsing, keep nozzle tips clear of mineral build-up to maintain an even spray pattern.

For other household cleaning projects where low pressure and controlled water flow matter, simple techniques often outperform complex machines. Just as [robotic window cleaners](/cleaning/are-window-cleaning-robots-worth-it/) fit specific flat glass panels rather than every home surface, or [portable evaporative coolers](/outdoor/do-portable-evaporative-coolers-work/) suit dry climates over humid rooms, a standard hose setup suits regular paver maintenance far better than high-pressure blasting.

Reseal your pavers every 3 to 5 years depending on sun exposure and rainfall. A quality breathable sealer protects the stone against oil absorption, retards weed growth, and makes future hose cleanings even easier.
