## The short answer

A garment steamer is the faster tool for most everyday wrinkles: one shirt on a hanger, a dress out of a suitcase, a sweater that got squashed in a drawer. An iron is the better tool when you want a crisp, pressed look or a sharp crease, and when you have a pile to get through.

If you only own one, choose based on what you wear. Mostly casual, soft, and unstructured clothes point to a steamer. Dress shirts and creased trousers every workday point to an iron. Good Housekeeping’s cleaning lab, which tests both, says having both gives you the most options.

Handheld 2-in-1 models, like the Rowelle portable steamer covered below, add a small heated plate so you can press a collar or hem without setting up a board. That closes part of the gap, not all of it. We have not tested Rowelle. What we say about it is based on the maker’s specs and on how this type of tool works.

## What steam does, and what pressure does

Wrinkles are fibers that have been bent and set in place. Heat and moisture relax them. Pressure and weight flatten them into a new shape.

A steamer supplies heat and moisture, and gravity does the rest while the garment hangs. Nothing presses the fabric flat, which is why steamed clothes look smooth but soft, and why steam is kind to textures like knits, velvet, and lace. That moisture is a burst on a shirt. A [portable evaporative cooler](/outdoor/do-portable-evaporative-coolers-work/) adds moisture to a whole room for hours, and it only helps when the air is dry.

An iron supplies heat, often steam, and a lot of pressure through a wide, hot plate. That is how you get a crease that stays put. It is also how you flatten a texture you wanted to keep or put a shine on dark fabric.

A 2-in-1 handheld does a little of both. It steams a hanging garment, and its small plate can press a strip of fabric against something firm. The plate is the size of a palm, not a full soleplate, and you supply the pressure.

## When a steamer wins

- **One item, right now.** No board to set up, no waiting for a big iron to heat. You steam the shirt on the hanger and leave.
- **Knits, soft dresses, and casual clothes.** Good Housekeeping lists knits, soft casual garments, and pieces with sequins or beads among the steamer’s strengths.
- **Awkward shapes.** Shoulders, puffed sleeves, and gathered skirts are easier with a tool that comes to the fabric than with a board.
- **Textures you do not want flattened.** Velvet, corduroy, and lace do better with steam than with an iron, per Good Housekeeping.
- **Travel.** A compact handheld fits in a bag. A full iron does not.
- **Curtains and drapes,** which you can freshen while they hang.

## When an iron still wins

- **Crisp creases and pleats.** Good Housekeeping notes that steamers can struggle with creases, pleats, and stubborn wrinkles. An iron is built for exactly those.
- **Dress shirts that must look pressed.** Collar points, a placket, and a flat front look sharper under pressure.
- **Batch work.** Irons carry larger tanks, and Good Housekeeping found the best-performing models draw about 1,700 to 1,800 watts.
- **Sewing and quilting,** where you press seams flat as you go.

## What not to steam

This is where people ruin clothes, so read the care label before you start. A crossed-out iron or steam symbol is your answer.

- **Leather and suede.** Better Homes & Gardens says never to steam them. They can warp, crack, stiffen, or water-spot. Good Housekeeping is a bit softer, saying to steam them very carefully if at all. Either way, a home steamer is a bad bet.
- **Waxed and coated fabrics,** including waxed jackets and water-resistant outerwear. Heat and moisture can melt or break down the coating.
- **Vinyl, and thin plastic trim or glued embellishments,** which steam can melt or loosen.
- **Sharp pleats.** Steam can relax them out of shape, especially in silk or chiffon.
- **Structured, tailored jackets** with fused interlining or shoulder pads. These can react unpredictably to heat and moisture, so a professional cleaner is the safer route.
- **Dirty or stained clothes.** Better Homes & Gardens lists them as well. Clean first, then de-wrinkle, because heat can set a stain.

For blends, follow the most delicate fiber. When in doubt, test steam on an inside seam first.

- **Steam and dry press in one handheld (per the maker),** so small pressing jobs do not need a board.
- **Five steam levels plus dry heat,** which suits a closet of mixed fabrics.
- **Compact and light (1.26 lb, per the maker)**, which makes it easy to pack.

### Cons

- **1,200 W and a small plate.** It will not match a full iron on crisp creases.
- **Voltage is not specified for international travel.**
- **Unverifiable review numbers and awards** on the sales page.
- **No independent test of this model that we could find.**

## Who it is for, and who should skip it

**Good fit:**

- You mostly need to fix one or two items before heading out.
- You travel and want something that steams and also presses a collar.
- You wear knits, soft dresses, and casual clothes more than crisp business wear.
- You live in a small space and do not want an ironing board out.

**Skip it, or keep your iron:**

- You press several dress shirts and creased trousers every week.
- You sew or quilt.
- Much of your wardrobe is leather, suede, waxed cotton, or tailored jackets. Steam is the wrong tool for those anyway.

## How to steam a shirt without spots or shine

General technique for handheld steamers, not results from a test:

1. **Read the care label** and pick the setting for the most delicate fiber in the blend.
2. **Fill only to the max line** and let the steamer heat fully. Spitting usually means it is not hot enough yet or it was overfilled.
3. **Hang the garment and pull it gently taut** from the hem with your free hand. Keep that hand away from the steam.
4. **Work top to bottom in slow, vertical strokes.** Hold the steamer upright if the manual says to, since some models leak when tilted.
5. **Press collars, cuffs, and the placket** with the plate against a firm, heat-safe surface. Use a pressing cloth over silk and synthetics, as Rowelle’s instructions advise.
6. **Let the garment cool and dry on the hanger** for a few minutes before wearing it, so the fibers set smooth.
7. **Unplug, cool, and empty the tank** after use.

A wrinkle and a dirty patio are different cleaning jobs. The guide on [garden-hose pressure nozzles](/cleaning/garden-hose-pressure-nozzle/) is about the rinse. The liquid in a steamer tank is water, not the mix from [cleaning tablets](/cleaning/do-cleaning-tablets-replace-spray-bottles/), which belongs on a counter you can wipe.
