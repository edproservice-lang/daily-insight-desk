## The short answer

A garden-hose “pressure” nozzle works when the job is a rinse. It puts the water you already pay for into a shape you can aim: a fan for a patio, a stream for mud on a boot, a softer spray for leaves on a chair. That is useful every week of the year.

It does not work when the ad is selling you a pressure washer. A pressure washer is a machine with a pump. The pump is the whole product. A nozzle on the end of a hose has no pump. It cannot raise the pressure your city, your well, or your home’s plumbing already delivers. If a page, including a page about a nozzle we link below, calls that attachment a pressure washer, read the claim as marketing.

We have not stood in a driveway with a gauge and compared brands. The distinction does not require that test. One tool adds energy. The other only organizes the flow.

## A nozzle shapes water. It does not make pressure.

Open the spigot with no nozzle and the hose dumps a soft column. Thread a nozzle on and you ask the same water to leave through a smaller opening. The stream moves faster and it covers less ground. That is why it feels “stronger” in your hand, and why it knocks loose grit off a paver that an open hose only darkens.

Two limits travel with that trick.

First, you did not create new pressure at the spigot. Homes differ. A house at the end of a rural line, a house with an old galvanized run, or a spigot fed by a long, skinny, kinked hose will feel weak no matter how clever the tip is. A nozzle cannot repair the supply. If the un-nozzled hose already fills a bucket slowly, no attachment will behave like a washer.

Second, a faster stream is a smaller stream. You trade coverage for impact. That is the right trade for rinsing a chair leg. It is the wrong trade if you were hoping to strip a deck or cut through packed mud the way a rented washer does. People who are disappointed in nozzles are usually people who bought the trade without noticing it.

Distance matters as much as the setting. Impact falls off as you step back. A jet that stings your hand at six inches is a ordinary rinse at a few feet. Start farther away than you think, especially on wood, soft mortar, window screens, and anything painted.

## What a focused hose stream is good at

Use the hose, with a nozzle you can shut off in your hand, for the mess that is loose.

- Patio and walkway dust, pollen, and the crumbs of a weekend. Wet it, and the film moves.
- Outdoor furniture before anyone sits down: a fan of water across mesh, slats, and cushions that are labeled as rinseable. Check the tag on a cushion before you soak the filling.
- Mud on boots, a dog’s paws at the back door, and tools that came in from the beds. A stream knocks the soil off so you are not carrying it inside.
- A car that is dusty rather than filmed with road grime. Rinse from the top down, then wash with a mitt if you actually need it clean. The nozzle’s job is the loose layer and the soap rinse, not the bonded dirt.
- Siding and windows, as a rinse after you have done the washing with a brush and a cleaner made for that surface. The hose takes the soap away. It does not do the washing.

On all of these, a shutoff at the nozzle is the feature that matters more than any “turbo” engraving. You can walk to the next chair without turning the spigot, and you are not watering the foundation while you move a pot.

## What still needs a brush or a real pressure washer

A nozzle fails honestly at anything that has grabbed the surface.

Moss and algae in a shady joint have roots, or at least a grip. Water slides over the green and leaves it. A cleaner meant for the paver, a dwell time, and a stiff brush do the work. The hose comes back at the end to carry the mess into the yard, not into the beds if you can help it.

Oil, rust stains, and barbecue grease are the same story. They are not dirt sitting on top. A focused rinse can cool a grate and carry away flakes you have already scraped. It will not dissolve a black patch on concrete. That patch wants a degreaser labeled for concrete, or, if you care about the result enough to rent a machine, a real pressure washer used on a setting that will not etch the slab. More water from a hose is not a gentler version of that machine. It is a different tool.

Paint prep, gum, and decades of gray on a deck are pressure-washer jobs or sandpaper jobs. Pointing a household jet at them until you are bored does not change the category. It only soaks the wall behind them.

Be careful with force even when you stay with the hose. A tight stream driven into a lap of siding, under a door, or at a screen can push water where the house did not want it. Wood fibers fuzz. Soft mortar joints lose sand. If the surface is older than the rest of the patio, believe the surface.

## How to rinse a patio without a machine

This is the sequence that matches what a hose can do. It is not a product demo.

1. Move the pots, the mat, and the toys. Sweep or blow the loose leaves first. If you spray that layer, you make mud, and then you have a second job.
2. Choose a fan, not a needle, and stand back. Wet the area so the dust lifts instead of smearing.
3. If the pavers are only dusty, the rinse may be the whole task. Work from the high side, or from the house outward, so the dirty water leaves instead of pooling against the foundation.
4. If there is a film, algae shadow, or food stain, stop expecting the water to finish it. Use a cleaner that names your surface, give it the time the label asks for, scrub, and then rinse. The nozzle is the last step.
5. Keep the stream moving. Parking a jet on one brick, one board, or one window screen is how a rinse becomes damage.
6. Shut the nozzle off while you look. A hose left running “for a second” against a wall is a leak you are paying for.

When the patio is in full shade and stays damp, the green will return. That is a drainage and sunlight problem, not a nozzle problem. A future note on moss without a pressure washer is named on the [outdoor shelf](/outdoor/). The note itself is not a link yet.

## When hard water clogs the tip

If the spray used to be even and now it spits, fans sideways, or dribbles from the swivel, look at mineral scale before you look at a new tool. Hard water leaves a pale crust in the tiny openings. Sediment from an older hose does the same thing, and it often shows up the week you use the hose hard after a winter in the garage.

A common household fix, and one nozzle makers often describe in their own care notes, is to take the tip apart if it is designed to come apart and soak the crusted pieces in white vinegar. Vinegar is an acid. It loosens the mineral film so you can brush it out of the holes with an old toothbrush and flush them. Leave it until the crust softens. There is no universal minute count, because the crust is not universal. Rinse the parts before they go back on the hose.

Read the fitting’s own note if it has a coating, a rubber seal, or a filter the maker says not to soak. Do not mix vinegar with bleach, and do not mix it with another cleaner “to speed it up.” If you move on to a labeled descaler because vinegar did not touch the crust, follow that label and only that label.

Before you blame the nozzle, flush the hose itself. Disconnect it, aim it at the lawn, and let it run long enough to push out the grit that has been sitting in the low spot. Then check the washer inside the fitting. A missing or cracked washer makes a connection that spits at your shoes and starves the tip. That feels like low pressure. It is a leak.

If you are on a well, or you already know the house has low pressure indoors, a nozzle will not invent what the plumbing does not have. The honest shopping question is then “which pattern still rinses at the pressure I have,” not “which nozzle is really a washer.” That narrower guide is named on the [tools shelf](/tools/) and is not linked yet.

## How to choose without overbuying

Buy, or keep, a nozzle you can shut off at the handle, that does not leak at the threads, and that still produces an even fan after a season. Patterns are the feature. A mist, a fan, and a stream cover the real week. A “drill” or “turbo” setting is the one most likely to mark wood and mortar, and it is the one the ads lean on.

You do not need a new nozzle to rinse a patio this Saturday if the one in the bucket still sprays evenly. Clean the tip, replace the washer, and unkink the hose. Spend the money on a brush that fits the paver, which is the tool the water was never going to replace.

Rent or buy an actual pressure washer when the job is a pump job: a deck that has gone gray and you have decided to refinish it, concrete with set-in oil, or siding you have already accepted you will rinse very carefully according to the machine’s instructions. Renting for a day is often the better version of that decision. Owning a second machine to knock pollen off a chair is how garages fill up.
