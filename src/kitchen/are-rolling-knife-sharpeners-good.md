## The short answer

Rolling knife sharpeners are good at the one thing most home cooks get wrong: holding a steady angle. A magnetic base clamps the knife at a fixed angle, and you roll an abrasive disc along the edge. No freehand wobble, no soaking a stone, no learning curve measured in months.

That makes a rolling sharpener a genuine step up from the pull-through slot in a kitchen drawer, and a reasonable alternative to a whetstone for people who know they will never learn one. It is not magic. It is slower than a pull-through. It is less flexible than a set of stones. It struggles with very wide blades, and it cannot touch serrations.

This guide explains how the tool works, what an independent review of one actually found, and where the KatuSharp rolling sharpener sits in that picture. We have not tested KatuSharp. Everything we say about it comes from the maker’s specs and from how this type of tool works.

## How a rolling sharpener works

Sharpening means grinding a little steel off both sides of the edge until they meet in a clean, thin apex. The angle of each side, usually around 15 or 20 degrees, decides how keen and how durable that edge is. Keeping that angle the same on every stroke is the skill. It is why whetstones take practice.

A rolling sharpener splits the job into two parts:

1. **A magnetic angle block.** The flat of the blade snaps onto a wedge cut at a fixed angle, so the knife sits edge-up at, for example, exactly 20 degrees.
2. **A rolling abrasive disc.** A wheel with a diamond or similar abrasive face rolls on the counter beside the block. Its face meets the edge at the block’s angle. You roll it back and forth along the blade, then flip the knife and do the other side.

Because the angle is set by the block, not by your wrist, your hundredth stroke matches your first. That is the entire idea, and it is a good one.

### Why grit matters

Abrasives are graded by grit. Lower numbers are coarser and remove steel faster. Higher numbers are finer and leave a smoother, more polished edge. A dull or chipped knife needs a coarse grit to rebuild the edge. A knife that is only slightly dull needs a finer grit to refine it. Some rolling sharpeners ship with one abrasive plus a smooth honing side. Others, including KatuSharp, use two abrasive grits on one disc.

## Rolling vs. pull-through vs. whetstone

### Pull-through and electric sharpeners

You drag the knife through a V-shaped slot lined with abrasive, or through spinning wheels. It is fast and needs almost no skill. The trade-off, as Serious Eats’ Taylor Murray describes from kitchen experience, is that these tools can remove more metal than needed, which shortens a knife’s life over years of use. The fixed slot also does not adapt to the knife’s original edge angle.

### Whetstones

A whetstone can do everything: any angle, any grit, any shape of blade. It is also the slowest to learn. Most stones need soaking or lubricating, and a beginner tends to rock the angle and round the edge. Serious cooks still prefer stones, and for good reason.

### Rolling sharpeners

They sit in the middle. They take off less metal than a pull-through and need far less skill than a whetstone. They are less versatile than either in one way or another: slower than the slot, narrower than the stone. For a home cook with a block of ordinary stainless knives, that middle is often exactly the right place to be.

## What the reporting found

We did not run a sharpness test. Serious Eats did, on a different rolling sharpener. In March 2025, Taylor Murray reviewed the Horl 3 Cruise, a German rolling sharpener with a diamond disc, a honing side, and 15- and 20-degree magnetic angles. She measured edges with an edge tester before and after.

Her badly worn, chipped stainless knife read 1044 on that tester at the start. After about 10 minutes, roughly 100 passes per side, it was down to 600. With more time she got it to 239, which she described as about as sharp as a double-edged razor blade and most high-end knives out of the box. Lower is sharper on that scale.

Three points from that review apply to rolling sharpeners in general:

- **It works on a truly dull knife, but not quickly.** Chips take a lot of passes when the coarsest abrasive is not very coarse. A coarse whetstone would have been faster.
- **The magnet holds.** She reported that the magnetic block did not release the knife no matter how much pressure she used, which is the feature the whole design depends on.
- **Wide blades are awkward.** A large cleaver was too tall to meet the disc properly without improvising. Paring knives and ordinary chef’s knives were fine.

Her verdict: easier than a whetstone, no soaking and no mess, a clever solution for people who want better than a pull-through but will not master stones. It will never do what a full set of whetstones can.

## Pros and cons

### Pros

- **The angle is set for you.** This is the main reason to buy any rolling sharpener, and it is a good reason.
- **Two grits on one disc (per the maker),** so one tool can restore and then refine.
- **No water and no mess.** It works dry and lives on the counter or in a drawer.
- **Gentler on knives than a pull-through slot,** based on how the category works and what Serious Eats reported.
- **Replaceable disc (per the maker),** so wear does not mean replacing the whole tool.

### Cons

- **Slower than a pull-through,** especially on a neglected knife.
- **Two angles only.** If a knife was ground at 17 or 12 degrees, the first sharpening will reshape that bevel.
- **Not for serrated, ceramic, or single-bevel knives.** Serrated bread knives need a tapered rod. Ceramic blades will not hold on a magnet. Single-bevel Japanese knives are sharpened differently.
- **Wide cleavers are awkward** on rolling designs, per Serious Eats.
- **The finest grit is 1000.** That gives a good working edge. Enthusiasts who polish on finer stones will want to keep them.
- **Unverifiable marketing.** The rankings and review counts on the sales page come from the seller, not an independent source.

## Who it is for, and who should skip it

**Good fit:**

- You own a block of everyday stainless chef’s, utility, and paring knives and they are dull.
- You have tried a whetstone, or watched a video about one, and decided it is not for you.
- You currently use a pull-through sharpener and want something gentler on your knives.
- You want a sharpener that a second person in the house can use without a lesson.

**Skip it, or keep something else beside it:**

- Your most-used knife is a bread knife or another serrated blade.
- You use ceramic knives.
- You own single-bevel Japanese knives, or you enjoy whetstones and finishing an edge at very high grit.
- You need to fix big chips quickly. A coarse stone or a professional sharpener is faster.

## How to get a better edge from any rolling sharpener

These are general habits for the category, not results from a test.

- **Start coarse on a dull knife.** Stay on the coarse side until the edge catches lightly on a fingernail or bites a tomato skin along its whole length. Then refine. KatuSharp’s own troubleshooting says much the same.
- **Seat the blade flat on the magnet.** If the knife sits crooked, the angle is wrong no matter how carefully you roll.
- **Use one angle per knife.** Switching between 15 and 20 degrees means regrinding the bevel each time.
- **Even strokes, both sides.** Do the same number of passes on each side, and work heel to tip so the belly of the blade gets sharpened too.
- **Mind your fingers.** The edge faces up while you roll. Keep your hand on the disc, not near the edge, and move slowly at first.
- **Hone between sharpenings.** A few strokes on a honing rod straighten a rolled edge and stretch the time between real sharpenings.
- **Cut on wood or plastic.** Glass, stone, and metal boards undo the work fast. Our guide on [titanium cutting boards](/kitchen/do-titanium-cutting-boards-dull-knives/) explains why.
